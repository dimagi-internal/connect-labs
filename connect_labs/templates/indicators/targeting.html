Scoping to one country turns a continental scan into a country argument: the map, the table, the costing and the workings all narrow together.

Level is a separate fact from boundary depth, and the difference is the trap. Liberia has 136 districts on file and an ORS reading for none of them — every district would inherit one of fifteen county figures and rank in fifteen flat ties, which looks like more detail and is the same information on a finer grid. The level buttons say how many units at each depth carry a reading of their own; prefer the deepest level that is measured.

Rank the parts stops a wholly-selected country collapsing into a single row. You cannot rank fifteen counties against each other if they have been added together first.

Indicators come in two kinds, and they ask opposite questions. A burden measure is worse when high, so a threshold selects the places above it. A coverage measure is worse when low, so the threshold selects the places below it — and the quantity worth funding is the number of people left unreached, not the coverage rate.

Units are not interchangeable either: mortality is quoted per 1,000 live births, most other measures in percent.

The arithmetic is trivial once two things are fixed, and neither can be inferred from the data: what one unit costs, and what a unit is.

Which unit applies is a property of the programme — KMC is priced per newborn, a bednet per child, a water connection per household, a treatment per case — so it is chosen, not guessed.

Where an indicator implies no case count, the per-case basis is declined rather than approximated.

Areas above threshold

Rows sit at the coarsest unit that is honestly describable. Where every region of a country clears the threshold, the country appears as one row; where only some do, those regions appear individually. Counts on a country row are summed from its qualifying regions, never read from a separate national figure, so a country total can never disagree with the regions beneath it.
